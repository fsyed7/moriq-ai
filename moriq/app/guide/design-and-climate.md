# MORIQ AI starter guide: design and climate

AI-written general guidance. Not a MORIQ document, not a code source and not professional advice.

## Courtyards and passive cooling
A courtyard works as a shaded outdoor room and a ventilation device. In hot-dry climates, a compact, deep court keeps cool night air and limits direct sun, and planting or water adds evaporative cooling when the air is dry. In hot-humid climates, evaporation helps far less, so priority shifts to shade, air movement and avoiding trapped humid air. Check court proportion against sun path, openings on two or more sides for airflow, and how heat stored in paving releases at night.

## Hot-dry and composite climates such as inland India
Protect west and east facades, which take low-angle sun, and use deep overhangs on south faces in the northern hemisphere. Thermal mass can delay daytime heat when nights cool down. Plan for monsoon rain as well as summer heat: drainage, sheltered openings and materials that tolerate wetting and strong sun. Use local climate data rather than a rule of thumb before making any performance claim.

## Hot-humid coastal climates such as the Gulf
Expect long cooling seasons, salt-laden air and limited night cooling, so shade and airtight, well-insulated envelopes often matter more than thermal mass. Consider condensation risk at cooled interiors and glazing, corrosion of metal fixings near the coast, and sand or dust at openings. Choose coastal-grade materials and confirm durability with suppliers.

## Shading and glazing
Exterior shading stops heat before it reaches the glass, so it usually outperforms internal blinds. Horizontal shades suit high sun, and vertical fins or screens suit low east and west sun. Screens such as timber, metal or masonry jali can balance privacy, airflow and glare. Test glazing choices for both heat gain and daylight, since very dark glass can raise artificial lighting use.

## Daylight and natural ventilation
Daylight reaches further from high openings, and light-coloured reveals and ceilings help distribute it. Cross ventilation needs an inlet and outlet on different pressure zones with a clear path between them. Stack ventilation uses a tall space or shaft to draw warm air upward. Check security, rain, dust, noise and privacy, since these often decide whether occupants actually open windows.

## Vaastu in residential planning
Vaastu is a traditional planning system that many clients value. Common conventions associate the north-east with entry, water or prayer, the south-east with the kitchen, and the south-west with the main bedroom, while interpretations differ between practitioners. Confirm the client's consultant or preferences early, because orientation drives the whole plan. Treat vaastu as a client brief requirement rather than a building code or a performance claim, and resolve conflicts with climate, site access and regulations openly.

## Narrow linear plots
With neighbours on the long sides, side windows give little view or privacy, so internal courts and skylights can bring in light and air. Stagger volumes to create courts, and plan circulation so it does not consume the width. Check setbacks, party wall conditions, parking and services routes early, since they quickly use up a narrow plot.

## Indoor and outdoor connection
Large sliding or folding openings work when floor levels, thresholds, drainage and insect or dust control are solved together. Continuous materials across a threshold strengthen the connection, but the exterior surface must still drain and resist slipping and weather. Shade the transition zone so it stays usable through the hot part of the day.

## Interior material and light strategy
Build a limited palette with contrast in texture, not only colour. Review samples together under the actual lighting, because colour temperature and direction change how stone, timber and fabric read. Layer ambient, task and accent lighting, and coordinate fixtures with ceilings, joinery and switching early. Check maintenance, wear and humidity for each material in its location.
