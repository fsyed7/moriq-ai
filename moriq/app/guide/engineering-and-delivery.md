# MORIQ AI starter guide: engineering and delivery

AI-written general guidance. Not a MORIQ document, not a code source and not professional advice. Structural and services design need the responsible engineer.

## Structural load path
Every load needs a continuous route to the ground: slab to beam, beam to column or wall, then to foundation and soil. Architects should watch for columns that stop without a transfer, large cantilevers, big openings near supports and loads from water tanks or planters. Raise these with the structural engineer early, since late changes are expensive. Lateral loads from wind and earthquakes also need a defined path, usually through walls or frames.

## Reinforced concrete basics
Concrete resists compression, and steel reinforcement resists tension. Beams and slabs carry bending and shear, and columns mainly carry compression. Cover to reinforcement protects steel from corrosion and fire, and it matters more near the coast. Exposed concrete depends on formwork quality, joint layout, mix consistency and curing, so agree sample panels and tie-hole patterns before work starts. In hot weather, plan for early curing to limit cracking.

## Foundations and soil
Foundation type depends on soil investigation, loads, water table and neighbouring structures. Do not assume bearing capacity from a nearby site. Coordinate level changes, basements, drainage and retaining walls with the engineer. Salts in ground or groundwater, common in coastal and desert areas, can attack concrete and steel, so ask for protection measures when the soil report shows them.

## MEP coordination
Coordinate mechanical, electrical and plumbing routes with structure and ceilings before finishing drawings. Common clashes include ducts against beams, drains needing falls through tight ceilings, and equipment without service access. Reserve shafts and plant space early, and check that sleeves through beams are approved by the structural engineer. Overlay drawings or use a federated model to find clashes before site does.

## HVAC and envelope
Reducing heat gain through shading, insulation and airtightness lowers the cooling load before equipment is chosen. Coordinate equipment locations for noise, maintenance, condensate drainage and appearance from roofs and courts. Provide fresh air deliberately in airtight homes. Check condensation where cold ducts and pipes pass through warm, humid spaces.

## Waterproofing wet areas and roofs
Treat waterproofing as a continuous system including falls, upturns, penetrations, drains and protection layers. Most failures happen at junctions: door thresholds, pipe penetrations and parapets. Test by flood or ponding before covering it, and record the result. Coordinate floor build-ups early, since falls and screeds affect levels and door heights.

## Roofs, parapets and rainwater
Move rainwater off the roof quickly with falls toward outlets, and provide an overflow in case an outlet blocks. A detailed parapet needs a sloped coping that sheds water inward or with a projecting drip so it does not streak the facade. Take the waterproofing up the parapet and protect its top edge under the coping or a flashing. Keep joints in copings sealed and movable. Confirm sizes and upstand heights with the waterproofing supplier and the applicable standard.

## Material junctions
Junctions decide durability and appearance. Where timber decking meets pebble paving or stone, check drainage beneath both, movement gaps, edge restraint, fixing method and a level change that is safe underfoot. Keep timber off wet ground and allow air beneath it. Where dissimilar metals meet, separate them to prevent corrosion. Draw the junction at large scale and check it can be built in sequence.

## Planting, water features and landscape
Check soil depth, drainage, root spread, irrigation and mature size before placing trees near structures. Retaining existing trees requires protecting roots from level changes and excavation. Water features need waterproofing, plant space, access, overflow and a safety review for children. Choose species for local climate and water availability, and confirm with a landscape specialist.

## Drawing set and sheet review
A typical set moves from site plan and floor plans to elevations, sections, schedules and details. Check that every plan matches sections, levels and grids, that door and window tags match schedules, and that dimensions close. Confirm structural and services drawings are coordinated and of the same revision. Each sheet needs a title, scale, north point where relevant, revision and date.

## Junior architect review checklist
Before issuing a drawing, confirm the revision and purpose, then check grids, levels and dimensions. Compare the plan with the section at every stair, ramp and level change. Check door swings, furniture clearance and accessibility. Look for consultants' latest information, unresolved clouds and notes copied from old projects. Ask a senior to review anything you cannot explain.

## Stairs, ramps and accessibility
Rise, going, headroom, handrails and landing sizes are set by the applicable code, so check adopted requirements rather than recalled numbers. Keep stair geometry consistent, since uneven steps are a common trip hazard. Check headroom in section, not only in plan. Consider older occupants and step-free routes even where a home has no legal requirement for them.

## Fire safety in concept design
Fire strategy covers escape routes, compartmentation, structural protection, surface materials and firefighting access. The applicable code and authority set requirements, and these vary by building type and jurisdiction. Raise it at concept stage, since escape distances and stair locations shape the plan. Use a qualified fire consultant where the building type or authority needs one.

## Specifications, BOQ and tender
Drawings show location and geometry. Specifications define quality, standards and workmanship, and a bill of quantities prices the work. Keep them consistent, and state which document governs if they conflict. Name allowances and provisional sums clearly. Compare tenders on scope and exclusions, not price alone.

## Site coordination, RFIs and submittals
Use RFIs to record questions and answers in writing, and review submittals against design intent before materials are ordered. Log each with dates and status, and flag anything affecting cost or time. Mock-ups help resolve finishes and junctions before full production. Keep site instructions traceable to the current drawing revision.

## BIM and CAD practice
Agree a shared setup before modelling: coordinates, levels, grids, units, naming and level of detail. Link consultant models rather than copying geometry, and run clash checks at agreed stages. Keep one source for each piece of information so schedules and drawings cannot disagree. Follow the project's own standard where one exists.
