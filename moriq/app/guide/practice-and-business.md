# MORIQ AI starter guide: running projects and the practice

AI-written general guidance. Not a MORIQ document or legal, financial or professional advice. It describes common practice, not how MORIQ works.

## Project stages
Projects commonly move through brief, concept, schematic design, design development, authority approval, working drawings, tender, construction and handover. Each stage should end with a client decision recorded in writing. Changes get more expensive with each stage, so raise uncertainty early. Agree what each stage delivers before starting it.

## Client brief
Record who will live in or use the building, how they gather, privacy needs, future growth, cultural requirements such as vaastu, budget, timeline and who makes decisions. For family homes, ask each generation separately where you can. Turn the brief into a room list with areas and adjacencies, and have the client sign it off. Revisit it when the design changes direction.

## Site analysis
Before planning, record the survey, title boundaries, orientation, sun, wind, views, neighbours, road access, levels, trees, soil, water table and services. Note noise and privacy issues on site, not only from maps. Check the permitted land use and rules early. A site visit at different times of day often changes the design.

## Fees and appointment letters
A written agreement should set out scope, stages, deliverables, fee, payment schedule, number of revisions, extra-work rates, consultants included, ownership of drawings, termination and liability. Fees are commonly a percentage of cost, a lump sum or time-based, each with different risks. Tie payments to stage completion. Have a lawyer check the standard form, and check any professional body guidance.

## Consultant team
Homes and hotels typically involve structural, MEP, landscape, lighting, acoustic, kitchen, pool and façade specialists. Agree who appoints and pays each one, and who coordinates. Issue a responsibility matrix so nothing falls between scopes. Share a drawing register, and agree which revision each party is working from.

## Budget and cost control
Set a budget early with a contingency, and check it at every design stage against current rates. Imported finishes and furniture add lead time, currency and customs risk. Value engineering should protect what matters most to the client, rather than cutting evenly. Record every approved change with its cost.

## Programme and scheduling
List long-lead items such as imported stone, sanitaryware, glazing, lifts and furniture, and work back from the site date. Identify decisions the client must make and by when. Keep approvals, procurement and construction on one programme. Review it regularly, and tell the client early about slippage.

## Contractor selection and contracts
Prequalify contractors on similar work, team and finances. Issue the same documents to every bidder. Compare scope, exclusions and programme as well as price. The contract should cover payment, retention, defects, delays, variations and insurance. Have a lawyer review it, since local law decides what is enforceable.

## Site supervision and quality
Visit regularly at key stages such as reinforcement before pouring, waterproofing before covering and first-fix services before closing walls. Record observations and instructions in a site report with photographs. Check set-out against drawings before work advances. Use mock-ups for finishes, and do not approve work you have not seen.

## Snagging and handover
Inspect room by room, list defects with photographs, and track them to closure. Test services, doors, drainage and waterproofing before handover. Collect as-built drawings, warranties, manuals and maintenance guidance. Agree the defects period, and record its end date.

## Client communication and approvals
Send meeting minutes with decisions, actions and dates within a day or two. Get approvals in writing, including on samples and changes. Explain trade-offs in plain language with cost and time effects. Keep one point of contact where you can. Written records protect both client and practice when memories differ.

## Drawing and file management
Use a consistent project folder, file naming and revision system, and keep a drawing register showing what was issued, to whom and when. Archive superseded drawings rather than deleting them. Back up project files and limit who can issue. Follow the office's own standard where one exists.

## Client confidentiality and photography
Keep client names, addresses and floor plans confidential unless the client agrees. Get written consent before publishing a home, and check who owns photographs and what use the photographer's agreement allows. Do not paste confidential client details into external tools without checking they are approved.

## Mentoring junior staff
Give juniors a clear task, the reason behind it and a date for review. Have them walk a senior through their drawing, since explaining reveals gaps. Take them to site early, because seeing work built teaches detailing faster than drawings do. Encourage questions, and review mistakes without blame.
